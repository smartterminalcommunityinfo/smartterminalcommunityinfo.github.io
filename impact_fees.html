MONEY & POLITICAL INFLUENCE

Impact Fees & the 2019 Rate Freeze

How San Marcos calculates wastewater impact fees, why the City just proposed to roughly quadruple them, and why the SMART Terminal / TriTexas Logistics Park development agreement locks the developer into paying 2019-era prices instead.

Important disclaimer: This analysis is built from official public records - the City of San Marcos impact fee ordinance (Secs. 86.294 through 86.299), the City's March 2026 Wastewater Impact Fee Final Report, the executed development agreement, and released Governor's Office RFI correspondence. One figure in the City's own cost report did not internally reconcile (flagged below in the caveats section) - the dollar comparisons here should be read as directionally solid, not audit-grade.
The bottom line
≈ $17.9 million
The estimated gap between what SMART's development agreement locks it into paying in wastewater impact fees, and what the City's own 2026 study says that same amount of sewer capacity actually costs to provide today.

Impact fees

An impact fee is a one-time charge the City assesses against new development to recover the cost of the water/sewer capital infrastructure that development's demand requires - pipes, lift stations, treatment capacity - rather than spreading that cost across the existing ratepayer base.

How the City converts demand into LUEs (Secs. 86.294, 86.298, 86.299)

Every impact fee is the product of two numbers: a count of service units (LUEs), and a dollar rate per LUE. The development agreement freezes the rate. The count is governed by the City's ordinance, and the method the ordinance uses turns out to matter as much as the frozen rate does.

  1. One LUE is 0.41 gpm. Sec. 86.294 defines a service unit as a living unit equivalent "based upon the relationship of the maximum design water flow to that of single family residential peak demand as served by a [fraction]-inch displacement-water meter," and fixes that single-family peak demand at 0.41 gpm.
  2. The input is peak water flow, calculated from plumbing fixtures. Sec. 86.294 defines "peak flow calculation" as "the calculation of water demand based upon the proposed fixture types using city approved methodology." Under Sec. 86.299(b)(1)(a), where industrial or commercial equipment makes a fixture count unworkable, water demand comes from manufacturer data and is added to the fixture-count flow.
  3. Wastewater LUEs are derived from the water number. Schedule 1 has a single input column, "Calculated Peak Water Flow (gpm)," and both the water utility and wastewater utility fee tiers hang off it. The sewer fee is not based on measured or projected sewer discharge.
  4. The applicant produces the calculation. Under Sec. 86.299(b)(1), peak water demand "is to be determined initially by the applicant and is subject to review and approval by the director," who may require building plans, plumbing layouts and flow calculations.
When the count is fixed, and at what rate. Sec. 86.298(a)(2) provides that "for a new development on platted property the assessment shall occur at the time of plat recordation, and shall be the maximum amount of the impact fee per service unit in effect for the date of recordation." Sec. 86.298(a)(1) supplies a different rule, assessment at building or plumbing permit, but only where "platting is not required" – and Section 1.10 of the development agreement is titled "No Waiver of Platting Requirements" and confirms that nothing in it waives the platting requirements for public improvements. So Sec. 86.298(a)(2) is the operative provision here, and no plats have been recorded, which means no assessment has yet occurred. Sec. 86.298(b) adds that once assessed, the per-unit amount cannot be increased unless the quantity of service units increases, in which case "a new assessment shall occur using the Schedule 1 rate then in effect for such additional service units."

The consequence is that identical demand produces very different fees depending on how the development is divided into connections. Why this matters beyond this project. The step table applies to every development in San Marcos and is a feature of the current code, not something negotiated in the development agreement. It also interacts with the freeze: a ceiling expressed in LUEs only constrains a project if the charged LUE count actually reaches it, and this table is structured to keep charged counts low. The rate schedule now before Council is the occasion to examine it.

Applied to this project, the City's own rule points one way. No plats have been recorded for the TriTexas / SMART Terminal property. Under the rule staff stated twice at this meeting, a development with no recorded plat before October 1, 2026 does not keep the current rates. Whether the development agreement's Section 1.05.A.3 displaces that outcome is the open question set out in the next section, but the City's stated default rule and the agreement's text now point in opposite directions on the record. Staff have since answered which one they consider controlling, and the answer is the agreement. See The City's stated position below.

The freeze was not discussed

No speaker, Council Member or staff member mentioned the SMART Terminal, Axis, or TriTexas project, Franklin Mountain, or the development agreement's frozen 2019 rate at any point in the item. The public comment and Council discussion concerned housing affordability, homebuilders, and the Kissing Tree subdivision. The single largest frozen-rate exposure in the city does not appear in the record of the meeting that quadrupled everyone else's fee.

One exchange is a pointed contrast. Jeff Howard, an attorney representing Brookfield Residential, proposed among other options "allowing prepayment at current fees to help the city's near-term revenue." Other developers were at the podium asking to be allowed to lock in the 2018 schedule. This project's agreement asserts a lock at the 2019 schedule, which is the same numbers, without prepaying anything.

The SB 1883 audit does not reach contractual rate freezes

Item 8 of the same meeting received the independent financial audit newly required by Senate Bill 1883, performed by JMS CPAs and dated February 24, 2026. It is a statutory precondition rather than a formality: Tex. Loc. Gov't Code §395.059(a) provides that "before a political subdivision may increase an existing impact fee... the political subdivision must conduct an independent financial audit." The City appears to have satisfied the procedural requirements, including §395.059(b)(2)'s rule that the auditor not have been under contract for other services in the preceding 12 months.

The minutes summarize the engagement as having "examined collected fees, accrued interest, expended funds, waived fees, refund requests, and any errors or omissions dating back to 2018," with findings confirming "full compliance." That is accurate for what was examined. The report itself is narrower than the summary in two ways that matter here.

First, the waiver procedure covers affordable housing only. Procedure 6 of the report reads: "To determine the amount of impact fees waived under Section 395.016(g)..." Section 395.016(g) is the affordable-housing waiver, and it is the only waiver authority Chapter 395 contains. The scope is set by statute: §395.059(c)(7) requires an accounting of "any waived impact fees in the service area under Section 395.016(g)," and nothing broader. Total waivers identified across the entire April 17, 2018 through September 30, 2025 period were $382,479.

A rate frozen by contract is not a §395.016(g) waiver. It therefore falls outside the audit by legislative design rather than by any oversight on the part of the City or the accountants. The consequence is that nothing in the statutory audit framework requires a city to quantify contractual rate commitments before raising its fees, and nothing in the August 5 record does so.
Second, it is an agreed-upon procedures engagement, not an audit. The report states that JMS "were not engaged to and did not conduct an audit or review engagement, the objective of which would be the expression of an opinion or conclusion," and that "had we performed additional procedures, other matters might have come to our attention." It also states it "is intended solely for the information and use of the management of the City of San Marcos... and should not be used by anyone other than these specified parties." The agenda title's phrase "Independent Financial Audit" is the statute's own language, but the instrument is narrower than the phrase suggests.

The report does supply useful totals for the period: $44,514,174 in impact fees collected and $5,088,969 in accumulated interest, against $26.6 million in collected impact fees still unspent as of September 30, 2025 ($15.8 million water, $10.8 million wastewater). It also lists unconstructed capital projects funded by impact fees, totaling $6.85 million on the water side and $54.4 million on the wastewater side. Two of those unconstructed projects recur elsewhere in this research: the Gary Job Corps Lift Station (LS #46) decommissioning, which the SMART Development FM 110 Gravity Main is intended to accomplish, and the Highway 80 Lift Station Expansion, on which the wastewater service timeline for prospective tenants depends.

What is still open before the second reading

Council did not treat the first reading as final on details. Council Member Gonzalez asked whether Council could adopt the maximum rate now and add credits later, and City Attorney Aguirre said yes. Council Member Rodriguez asked staff to explore incentives for affordable housing, infill and green building before the second reading. Mayor Hughson stated that "tonight's vote is for what is in the packet with the understanding that possible amendments, incentives, and credit structures will be explored before the second reading on September 1, 2026." Two speakers asked for a tiered or service-area-based structure rather than a flat citywide fee.

What happens above 2,108 LUEs

If the site's charged LUE count passes the number the agreement reserves, does the 2019 rate still apply to the excess? Three sources speak to this, and they do not align.

Where the 2,108 came from, and what happened to it

The figure is not an engineering projection of what this site needs. Section 1.07.D.3 of the agreement reads:

"The City confirms that, as originally allocated in the Cotton Center Development Agreement, Owner shall be entitled to use of 2,108 LUEs of Wastewater Service on the Property."
Development Agreement, Section 1.07.D.3

So the number is an allocation inherited from a different agreement covering different land, carried across when Franklin Mountain's acreage moved out of Cotton Center and into this agreement. That explains why it does not correspond to any demand calculation for this project: it was never sized against one.

The agreement it was allocated under no longer exists. The Cotton Center Development Agreement was restated in January 2023, restated again effective June 7, 2024 (where Section 2.04 confirms Franklin Mountain is no longer an Owner, Primary Owner or Party to it, and which allocated 7,530 wastewater LUEs across the remaining Cotton Center sub-districts), and then terminated outright on November 5, 2025 by Res. 2025-212R, which also released roughly 1,786 acres from the City's ETJ. Section 1.07.D.3 is drafted as a standalone confirmation by the City rather than as a condition, so the better reading is that the entitlement survives inside this agreement on its own terms. But the capacity accounting that produced the number is gone, and no document in the record re-derives 2,108 against this project's own demand.

The City's ordinance: additional units get the current rate

Sec. 86.298(b) is the most direct answer available, and it is mandatory:

"Following assessment under subsection (a), the amount of the assessment per service unit for that development cannot be increased unless the quantity of service units needed for that development increases by the submission of a new application for plat approval or other development application that results in approval of additional service units. In the event of such an increase in service units, a new assessment shall occur using the Schedule 1 rate then in effect for such additional service units."
San Marcos Code of Ordinances Sec. 86.298(b)

Read on its own, that means LUEs beyond the assessed or agreed quantity are charged at whatever rate is current when they are assessed. At the proposed schedule that is $11,161 per wastewater LUE rather than $2,684, a difference of $8,477 per LUE.

State law: the same direction, less explicitly

Texas Local Government Code Sec. 395.017 provides that after assessment of impact fees "or execution of an agreement for payment of impact fees," fees may not be increased "unless the number of service units to be developed on the tract increases," and that in that event "the impact fees to be imposed are limited to the amount attributable to the additional service units."

Two things follow. Sec. 395.017 does not itself name a rate; the rate comes from Sec. 395.016(d) and Sec. 86.298(a), which fix it at the time of assessment. And more importantly, Sec. 395.017 is the provision that gives an assessment or an agreement its lock-in effect in the first place, and it carves additional service units out of that protection on its face. A freeze that depends on Sec. 395.017 cannot escape Sec. 395.017's own limitation.

The agreement: no ceiling at all

The development agreement's own language points the other way, and it is broad:

"Due to the significant investment undertaken by Owner in developing the Property, the City agrees that all water and wastewater impact fees to be charged by the City during the development of the Property will be the fees in effect as of March 19, 2019."
Development Agreement, Section 1.05.A.3

No service-unit limit, no acreage limit, no end date. The 2,108 LUE figure appears in a separate provision reserving sewer capacity, not as a cap on the freeze. So the ceiling, if there is one, comes from the law rather than from the contract, and the owner's position will be that Sec. 1.05.A.3 covers every fee the City ever charges on this land.

The City's stated position

Asked directly which controls where an agreement and the Code point in different directions, Assistant Director of Engineering Richard Reynosa answered in writing:

"An agreement which establishes a set impact fee on a specific date governs over the existing City code and future amendments."
City staff response to written questions, August 28, 2026

That is the City's answer to the question this section poses, and it is broader than the question required. It concedes both Sec. 86.298(b) as it stands and Ordinance 2026-29 itself, and it is not limited to this development. It came from engineering staff rather than from the City Attorney, so it states the City's operating position rather than a legal opinion, and it does not resolve the state-law question below. But the City's default rule and the City's position on this agreement can no longer both be described as undetermined.

Why the answer is still not settled

A contract term may well displace a city ordinance, so Sec. 86.298(b) alone does not end the argument. What a contract cannot do is give the City authority the Legislature withheld, which is why the state-law question is the one that matters. And the authority for an impact fee agreement, Sec. 395.018, is narrow in two ways at once:

"A political subdivision is authorized to enter into an agreement with the owner of a tract of land for which the plat has been recorded providing for the time and method of payment of the impact fees."
Tex. Loc. Gov't Code Sec. 395.018

No plats have been recorded for this property. And the authority granted reaches the time and method of payment, not the amount. Whether the general authority in Sec. 212.172(b)(9) to include "other lawful terms" in a development agreement absorbs a rate freeze that Chapter 395 does not itself authorize is an open question, and Sec. 395.073 provides that state and local restrictions on impact fees are cumulative rather than displaceable. This page sets out the texts; it does not resolve them.

The practical catch

A ceiling measured in LUEs may never be reached, whatever the law says. Because of the Schedule 1 step table, a project built out as many separately permitted connections generates a far smaller charged LUE count than the same demand delivered through one large connection. Actual demand on the treatment plant could run several times the reserved capacity while the charged count stays under 2,108. The service-unit ceiling protects the City only if the charged number crosses it.

What could be settled now, without litigation

Sec. 86.297(b) requires the fee per service unit to be "established by ordinance of the city council," and Ordinance 2026-29 has passed only its first reading. The second reading is set for September 1, 2026, and the Mayor has already stated that amendments, incentives and credit structures are open for discussion before it. Staff have now given their answer, so the remaining question is narrower: whether the City Attorney and Council adopt that position, and whether it extends to service units beyond the reserved quantity. An answer either way is useful: it bounds the City's exposure, or it puts a current position on the record rather than one taken in 2023.

Driver of the 2026 rate increase

The March 2026 Wastewater Impact Fee study lists the specific capital projects the updated rate is designed to fund. One of them serves this development directly:

SMART Development (FM 110) Gravity Main - a new 18-inch sewer line along FM 110 that also decommissions the aging Gary Job Corps lift station. Total estimated cost: $24,078,600. The City's own utilization study attributes 63% of this project's need to growth between 2025–2035, growth the study ties explicitly to the proposed SMART development.

The project detail sheet states the driver plainly: "This project is primarily driven by the proposed SMART development and extends service to the HC-04 subbasin." So the cost of serving this development is inside the fee now being adopted.

How the $11,161 was calculated, and what it assumes

Table ES-3 of the study sets out the arithmetic:

Service units are projected to rise from 34,348 in 2025 to 60,823 in 2035, a compound annual growth rate of 5.88 percent, and the 26,475-unit difference is the denominator.

The 2,108 reserved units are about 8 percent of that denominator. At $2,684 rather than $11,161 they yield roughly $5.7 million instead of $23.5 million. The resulting shortfall of about $17.9 million is a little over 6 percent of the $295,489,198 the fee is designed to recover.

Neither study mentions the development agreement, frozen rates, or grandfathering anywhere. That is not an error. Chapter 395 prescribes a maximum allowable fee computed as eligible cost divided by service units, and the study performs that calculation correctly. It is not asked to model what the City will actually collect. The consequence is simply that no document in the record sets the adopted rate against projected actual recovery.

The City confirmed this in writing on August 28, 2026. Asked whether the record contains any analysis of projected actual recovery as distinct from the maximum allowable fee, Assistant Director of Engineering Richard Reynosa responded that the method determines "the maximum allowable fee based on projected costs and growth over the next 10 years," and that "actual recovery or agreements made are not factors accounted for in order to provide an equal and proportionate assessment of the fee." Asked separately whether the City maintains an inventory of contractual rate commitments and has estimated their long-term cost, the response was: "There is not an inventory of these contractual rate commitments." So the gap described above is not merely unpublished. It has not been calculated, and the City does not hold the list of agreements that would be needed to calculate it, here or anywhere else in the system.

One methodological note worth flagging. The study converts connections to service units by meter size (Table 2-2), while Code Schedule 1 converts by calculated peak water flow. Those are different methods, and whether they reconcile is not addressed in the study. It may be nothing more than the difference between aggregate planning and individual assessment.

Funding the gap

Impact fees are one of the primary funding sources for growth-driven infrastructure such as the SMART Development Gravity Main. A shortfall between the fee collected and the study's cost basis doesn't eliminate the need to build the infrastructure. Under standard municipal utility financing, such a gap is typically covered by some combination of:

  • Existing utility ratepayers, through water/sewer bills
  • City debt (bonds), repaid over years by the utility system as a whole
  • General utility revenue that could otherwise fund other system needs or rate relief

This page does not have documentation showing which of these the City intends to use - that would require the City's specific CIP funding/financing plan, which wasn't in the records reviewed here.

Caveats

  • Both the methodology and the 2,108 figure are now sourced to primary documents. The conversion method (Secs. 86.294, 86.297, 86.298 and 86.299) has been read out of the City Code. The 2,108 LUE figure and the fee freeze have been read out of the executed development agreement itself, at Sections 1.07.D.3 and 1.05.A.3 respectively, replacing the earlier reliance on a summary. The executed PDF held in this project's records has no text layer, so those provisions were read from the page images rather than extracted as text.
  • The pipeline LUE estimates are demand-based, not fixture-count based. Sec. 86.299 computes LUEs from a plumbing fixture count, not from average daily demand. The estimates above convert stated gpd figures to gpm and read Schedule 1, which establishes order of magnitude but is not the calculation the City would perform. RFI water and wastewater figures are also company-stated projections at the inquiry stage, and several projects state figures for a first phase only.
  • The ordinance text has an illegible fraction. The copy of Sec. 86.294 obtained through public information request renders the residential meter size as "[fraction]-inch" in both places it appears. The 0.41 gpm peak-demand basis is legible and is the operative number.
  • The City's own cost report has an internal inconsistency. The itemized cost sheet for the SMART Development Gravity Main project (in the March 2026 report) totals $9,631,500 on its own line-item math, while the report's summary table lists the same project at $24,078,600 in three separate places. The same mismatch appears on an adjacent project's detail sheet, suggesting a row-reference/formatting error in how the source PDF was assembled - not something resolvable from the document alone. This page uses the $24,078,600 summary-table figure, which is corroborated in multiple places.
  • The 2026 rate schedule has passed one reading of two, and is not yet adopted. Ordinance 2026-29 passed first reading 6–1 on August 5, 2026, with the second reading set for September 1, 2026 and a proposed effective date of October 1, 2026. Council explicitly left amendments, credits and possible tiering open before the second reading, so the final adopted numbers could differ from the $14,519 and $11,161 figures used on this page.
